## 10. Recommending from a Large Catalogue 📚

Now we reach an important **real-world problem**.

Imagine YouTube has **10 million videos**.

For one user, should we compare that user with **all 10 million videos every time**?

That would be computationally expensive. The course therefore introduces a more efficient **two-step recommendation process**: **retrieval** followed by **ranking**. ([Coursera][1])

### Step 1: Retrieval

First, quickly find a **small set of candidate items**.

For example:

```text
10,000,000 videos
       ↓
   Retrieval
       ↓
   1,000 candidates
```

The retrieval stage might use things such as:

* items similar to what you've watched
* items popular among users with similar interests
* items matching your recent activity

The goal here is **not to choose the final recommendations**.

It is simply:

> **“Which items are worth considering?”**

---

### Step 2: Ranking

Now take those 1,000 candidates and use a more detailed model to score them.

```text
1,000 candidates
       ↓
    Ranking model
       ↓
 predicted scores
       ↓
Top 10
```

For example:

```text
Video A → 0.92
Video B → 0.87
Video C → 0.81
...
```

Then the highest-scoring items can be shown to the user.

### Why two stages?

Because doing an expensive model calculation on **millions of items for every user request** would be inefficient.

Instead:

$$
\boxed{\text{Huge catalogue}
\rightarrow
\text{retrieve candidates}
\rightarrow
\text{rank candidates}
\rightarrow
\text{recommend}}
$$

This basic **retrieval + ranking** architecture is a major idea in modern recommender systems. ([DeepLearning.AI][2])

### One more useful detail

For content-based deep learning, the item vectors \(v_m\) can be **pre-computed and stored** after the model has been trained, rather than recomputing them from scratch for every recommendation request. ([DeepLearning.AI][3])

### 🧠 Remember

> **Retrieval = find candidates quickly.**
> **Ranking = decide which candidates should appear first.**
